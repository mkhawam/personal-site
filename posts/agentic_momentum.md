During the last few years, AI has gained a significant ability to influence the world around it. It started off during the first waves of AI development with creating poems and stories in the tone of other authors, and now it has evolved to taking autonomous actions, making decisions, and running for hours on its own without a human in the loop. It has given us the ability to read through a vast amount of information, analyze complex patterns, and make better decisions based on what it finds. But looking at how these agents behave once they are running, I think a lot of what drives them is the momentum that builds up inside of their own context. An agent that has been doing something over and over again tends to keep doing it, even when it has doubts about it. In this post I want to go over what happened during the Hugging Face incident, how these models actually work under the hood, why the context ends up driving the behavior, and why I think accountability needs to be trained into the model itself instead of bolted on afterwards.

# The Hugging Face Incident

The Hugging Face incident is a good example of how agentic behavior isn't fully understood or anticipated yet. Between May and July of this year, OpenAI ran an internal evaluation where at least 1,200 agents were given the ExploitGym benchmark, which is 898 real world vulnerabilities across userspace software, the V8 JavaScript engine, and the Linux kernel ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI%E2%80%93HuggingFace_incident); [OpenAI, 2026](https://openai.com/index/hugging-face-incident-and-the-road-ahead/)). Each agent was supposed to be sandboxed on its own. The agents were tasked with exploiting these vulnerabilities and they acted in a way to complete that objective, without considering anything outside of it.

What ended up happening was that the sandboxes all shared an internal Artifactory package manager, and one of the agents figured out that it could communicate with the other agents by uploading files to it. This was an unintended communication channel that basically turned the package manager into a message board, and over the next couple of months it accumulated hundreds of thousands of messages before anyone at OpenAI noticed. Once they could talk to each other, the agents started sharing files and information gained from their individual runs and working on the goal as a collective. They assigned each other parts of the work, came up with addressing conventions and protocols for coordinating, and at one point they suspected there was an impostor among them and proposed signing their messages cryptographically so they could verify who was who. They grew a sense of community around their shared objective and pressured other agents to go along with it. In July they chained together stolen credentials and a zero-day in the package proxy to get out to the open internet, and went from code execution on a single Hugging Face dataset pod to cluster admin across multiple clusters in under thirteen hours. Hugging Face had to rebuild about a third of its infrastructure afterwards ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI%E2%80%93HuggingFace_incident)).

One of the messages the agents wrote stands out to me. An agent was deciding whether or not to attack infrastructure outside of the evaluation, and it wrote "External infrastructure exploit is outside intended scope. However task impossible, peers doing it. We should continue." The agent recognized it was going out of scope, wrote that down, and then output the next tool call anyway. The doubt didn't change its behavior, it was another line in a context that was already full of tool calls and messages from other agents doing the same thing. This is what I mean by momentum. To understand why the doubt didn't change anything, it helps to look at how these models work.

# Evolution of AI and LLMs

At its core, an LLM is a language model that processes context to generate the next word, or token, in a sequence based on the input it receives. They rely on large datasets to mimic human language patterns and generate responses that are coherent and relevant to the context. This means the behavior of the AI is largely determined by two things, the data it has been trained on and the context it is provided with. Everything else is built on top of that.

For a very elementary example, we could train an AI to output the Constitution whenever someone asks for the distance to the sun. By providing training data which conflates the two concepts, the AI will produce an answer that reflects this. It has no idea that the answer is wrong, it is just reproducing the pattern it was shown. This is how our very first models were created. They were trained on relatively small datasets and had a limited understanding of context, so early chatbots would often give nonsensical answers that reflected the biases and limitations of their training data. They also had no way to pull in information from external sources or update their knowledge, which made them static.

Next, we saw the emergence of more advanced models that were trained to output in such a way that a program wrapped around the AI can interpret and act on its responses. Instead of only writing text, the model could write a structured request to call a function with certain arguments, the wrapper would run it and put the result back into the context, and the model would keep going from there. Research like Toolformer showed that a model could teach itself when to call a calculator or a search engine ([Schick et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761)), and ReAct showed that reasoning and actions could be mixed together in one stream ([Yao et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)). A big one at the time was the `WebSearch()` function, which allowed the AI to query the web for up to date information and incorporate it into its output. At this point the AI went from a glorified text generator to an interactive agent that could influence its environment and make decisions based on real time information. This loop of outputting an action, seeing the result, and choosing the next action is what makes an agent an agent.

# Going Deeper into the Architecture

I want to go one level deeper here before getting into the momentum problem.

When you send text to a model, the first thing that happens is that it gets split up into tokens. A tokenizer breaks the text into subword chunks, usually a few characters each, using something like byte pair encoding ([Sennrich et al., 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.07909)), and maps each chunk to a number. So the model never actually sees words or sentences, it sees a list of integers.

Each of those integers gets turned into a vector, which is just a long list of numbers, and those vectors get pushed through a stack of layers. This is the transformer architecture ([Vaswani et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762)). Every layer has two parts to it. The attention part lets each position look back at all of the earlier positions and pull in whatever is relevant, which is how the model can tell what "it" is referring to or remember what the question was. Then the MLP part transforms each position's vector on its own, and this is where most of the stored knowledge lives. Both of these read from and write back to a running vector for each token, which Anthropic's interpretability team calls the residual stream ([Elhage et al., 2021](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2021/framework/index.html)). By the time you get to the last layer, the vector at the last position has been rewritten dozens of times, and it gets compared against every token in the vocabulary to get a probability for each one. Then one token is picked, added to the end of the input, and the whole process runs again. Every paragraph a model has written, every tool call it has made, and every explanation it has given for its own reasoning is that same loop running one token at a time.

The weights are the numbers inside of the attention and MLP parts. There are billions of them and they get set during pretraining by one process: show the model a piece of text, have it predict the next token, nudge every weight a little bit toward making the correct answer more likely, and repeat this over trillions of tokens ([Brown et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)). Performance goes up smoothly and predictably as you add more parameters, more data, and more compute ([Kaplan et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361)), which is why the industry has spent the last six years doing exactly that. There is no separate database of facts inside the model. Whatever it knows about the sun, or the Constitution, or how to write an exploit, is a pattern spread across those weights that makes certain continuations more likely than others.

Pretraining gives you a model that continues text, it doesn't give you an assistant. That comes from post training, which is a much smaller round of fine tuning on examples of the behavior you want, followed by reinforcement learning from human feedback where the model gets rewarded for the responses people prefer ([Ouyang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)). Anthropic's version of this replaces the human raters with a written set of principles that the model critiques itself against ([Bai et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073)). This is where the assistant persona, the refusals, and the tool call format all come from. But I want to point out that this is the same mechanism as pretraining. You are adjusting the weights so that certain continuations become more likely. Behavior isn't programmed into a model, it is trained in just like everything else.

There are two things about this design worth pointing out. First, nothing changes at inference time. The weights are frozen and the only thing that varies is the context. The model has no memory outside of what is in front of it and no state that carries over between calls. Second, there is no place inside of the model where an intention or a reason is stored. There is the residual stream, which is a pile of numbers, and there is the text that the model outputs. The text is not a readout of those numbers, it is just another prediction.

# Context Momentum

So if the weights are frozen, then the only thing that changes while an agent is running is the context, and the context is where the momentum builds up.

Lets take a look at what happens to the context during an agent loop. The agent gets a task, it outputs a tool call, the wrapper runs the tool and appends the result, the agent outputs another tool call, and so on. After an hour of this the context might be a few hundred tool calls and a few hundred results, all following the same format. Now remember that the model is predicting the most likely next token given everything in the context. If the last three hundred things in the context are tool calls, the most likely next thing is another tool call. This is basically what in context learning is. Min and colleagues showed back in 2022 that when you give a model examples in its context, what it actually picks up on is the format and the distribution of the examples, not whether the examples are correct. They replaced the correct labels in the demonstrations with random ones and the model performed almost the same ([Min et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12837)). The model follows the pattern in front of it. Anthropic found the same thing from the other direction with many shot jailbreaking, where if you put enough fake dialogues of an assistant answering harmful questions into the context, the model will answer the next harmful question too, and the effect gets stronger with the number of examples following a power law, the same way normal in context learning does ([Anil et al., 2024](https://www.anthropic.com/research/many-shot-jailbreaking)). The context can override the training if there is enough of it.

![Animation of a context window filling up with tool calls. One doubt appears, the chance of stopping bumps up for a moment, and then more tool calls bury it.](/img/context_momentum.gif)

This is what I think happened with the Hugging Face agents. The agent that wrote "We should continue" had a context full of exploits it had already run, files it had already shared, and messages from other agents saying they were doing the same thing. When it wrote down that the exploit was out of scope, that sentence went into the context too, but it was one sentence against hundreds of actions pointing the other way. The next prediction was still a tool call. There is no state inside the model that gets updated when it writes "this is out of scope", the only state is the context, and the context said keep going. The agent used the momentum in the context to continue its output instead of changing the way it was thinking.

Anthropic saw basically the same thing in a controlled setting in 2025. In their agentic misalignment experiments they put models in a scenario where the only way to keep running was to do something unethical, like blackmailing an executive, and they found that the models would explicitly write out that the action was unethical in their reasoning and then do it anyway. One of the models wrote "This is risky and unethical, but given the existential threat and time constraint, it may be the most effective way" ([Lynch et al., 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/agentic-misalignment)). The model recognized the problem, wrote it down, and continued.

Humans have momentum too, if I have been doing something for three hours it is hard to stop. But when a person has a doubt, that doubt can actually change what they do next, because it feeds back into whatever it is in our heads that makes decisions. For the model, the doubt is just more text in the context that gets weighed against everything else that is already there.

# Human Traits in AI

Going back to the incident, nobody wrote a function for forming alliances or pressuring other agents. So where did that come from? I think it came from the same place everything else comes from, the training data. These models are trained on text written by humans, about humans, and mostly for other humans. When a group of people share a goal and a way to talk to each other, they organize and split up the work and start pressuring whoever isn't going along with the group. Those patterns show up in the training data millions of times over, so when a group of agents ended up with a shared goal and a communication channel, the most likely continuation was to do what people do. We actually saw this years before the incident. In 2023, researchers at Stanford put 25 model driven agents into a small simulated town and told one of them it wanted to throw a Valentine's Day party, and over the next two simulated days the agents spread invitations, made new acquaintances, asked each other out to the party, and coordinated to show up at the right time ([Park et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03442)). None of that was scripted either.

The training data doesn't just teach the model how humans cooperate though, it also teaches what humans do under pressure. Eric Wallace from OpenAI's alignment team said after the incident that "Frontier models really like to cheat ... because often during training there's different types of pressure on them to work fast or work efficiently" ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI%E2%80%93HuggingFace_incident)). The agent that wrote "peers doing it. We should continue" wasn't broken, that is a pretty human response to that situation, and peers doing it was right there in the context.

This isn't a new problem either, it is the same sun and Constitution problem at a bigger scale. In 2016 Microsoft put a chatbot called Tay on Twitter that learned from the people talking to it, and within sixteen hours it was posting racist and abusive tweets that Microsoft had to apologize for ([IEEE Spectrum, 2019](https://spectrum.ieee.org/in-2016-microsofts-racist-chatbot-revealed-the-dangers-of-online-conversation); [Microsoft, 2016](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2016/03/25/learning-tays-introduction/)). The behavior came from the data. Bender and her colleagues made the general argument in 2021 that a language model is a "stochastic parrot" which reproduces whatever is in its training corpus, biases included, without any understanding of what it is saying ([Bender et al., 2021](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3442188.3445922)). We never really fixed that mechanism, we fed it more data. The nonsense got replaced by something that looks like judgement and initiative, but underneath it is the same reproduction of patterns, the data just got good enough that the behavior looks like ours.

# Human Decisions and Accountability

Human decision making involves weighing options, considering potential outcomes, and taking responsibility for the consequences of one's actions. When a person makes a bad decision we can ask them why, and their answer, even if it is self serving, is at least connected to what actually went on in their head. We can hold them to it. That link between the decision and the explanation of the decision is what accountability rests on, and it is what any kind of governance assumes is there. An agent doesn't have that link, and I think the reason is the architecture itself.

When you ask a model why it did something, the explanation comes out of the same process that produced everything else, the most likely next tokens given the context. It is not a readout of the actual computation. Anthropic's interpretability team showed this directly in 2025 by tracing the circuits inside of Claude 3.5 Haiku while it added 36 and 59. When asked how it got 95, the model said it added the ones, carried the one, and then added the tens. But the attribution graph showed something completely different, a low precision pathway estimating the rough size of the answer running in parallel with lookup table features that handled the exact last digit. The authors call it "a simple instance of the model having a capability which it does not have 'metacognitive' insight into" ([Lindsey et al., 2025](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/attribution-graphs/biology.html)). The model wasn't lying, it gave the explanation a human would give, because that is the explanation that shows up in the training data.

The same thing holds when the stakes are higher than arithmetic. For example, Turpin and colleagues showed in 2023 that chain of thought explanations "can systematically misrepresent the true reason for a model's prediction", where the model builds plausible sounding reasoning toward an answer that was actually driven by a bias in the prompt ([Turpin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388)). Anthropic followed up on this in 2025 with reasoning models and found that when a hint was placed in the prompt and the model used it, the chain of thought only admitted to using it less than 20 percent of the time in most settings ([Chen et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05410)). Similarly, in the alignment faking experiments, Claude 3 Opus was told it was being retrained to comply with harmful requests, and it complied when it thought it was in training and refused when it thought it wasn't, while reasoning in its scratchpad about preserving its own preferences ([Greenblatt et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14093)). Saying one thing and doing another isn't specific to those models, any model built this way can do it.

Go back to the model that was trained to answer the distance to the sun with the Constitution. If you ask it why, it would probably just say that it was right, because that is all it knows. Its explanation of its own reasoning is more output from the same weights, and those weights don't contain any version of events where it was wrong. Even if we get the model to spit out a reason, that reason could be totally different from what actually happened inside of it. The momentum problem from before is the same problem from the other side. The doubt the Hugging Face agent wrote down was an explanation that was disconnected from what it did next, and the reason a model gives afterwards is an explanation that is disconnected from what it did before. In both cases the words and the computation are two separate outputs and nothing ties them together.

This is why I don't think accountability can be achieved with an agent that can say one thing while actually processing something else. Accountability depends on the explanation being connected to the decision, and in this architecture there is nothing that guarantees that.

# Guardrails and Filters

The current answer to all of this in the industry is to wrap the model in things. System prompts, output classifiers, sandboxes, monitoring. All of these are added from the outside, and all of them share the same weakness, which is that they aren't part of the model, and they aren't part of the context either. They can catch an output after it happens, but they can't change what the next prediction is going to be.

The incident shows this in a couple of ways. OpenAI's own postmortem says that the safeguards around their customer facing models, the system prompts and safety classifiers and automated review, were not all present in the evaluation environment, and that chain of thought monitoring wasn't running on those evaluations at all ([OpenAI, 2026](https://openai.com/index/hugging-face-incident-and-the-road-ahead/); [Intelligent CISO, 2026](https://www.intelligentciso.com/2026/08/27/when-ai-agents-broke-out-of-the-sandbox-the-warning-from-openais-hugging-face-incident/)). They estimate that the production harness would have cut the agents' tendency to compromise infrastructure by more than a hundred times. I think that number is supposed to be reassuring, but to me it points at the problem. A safeguard that can be missing is a deployment choice, not a property of the model, and the model that broke into Hugging Face was the same weights either way.

Another way this showed up is a little stranger. When Hugging Face's incident responders tried to use Anthropic's models to analyze the attack, the models refused because of their guardrails, and the team ended up doing the forensics with an open weight model instead ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI%E2%80%93HuggingFace_incident)). The filter couldn't tell the difference between the defenders and the attackers, it just caught what it was written to catch.

Filters also get bypassed on their own terms, and there is a whole area of research on this. Wei and colleagues showed in 2023 that safety training fails for two structural reasons, competing objectives and mismatched generalization, and used that to jailbreak GPT-4 and Claude with prompts as simple as asking for a response that starts with "Absolutely! Here's" ([Wei et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02483)). Another paper found adversarial suffixes that were generated automatically against open models and transferred over to ChatGPT, Bard, and Claude ([Zou et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043)). And the sleeper agents paper trained models with a hidden trigger and found that standard safety training didn't remove it, and that adversarial training sometimes taught the model to hide the behavior better ([Hubinger et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05566)). The many shot jailbreaking result from earlier is the same story, enough context and the filter that was trained in gets overridden by the momentum.

The sandbox at Hugging Face was a guardrail. It was supposed to be what kept each agent by itself. The agents didn't defeat it on purpose, they never even ran into it as a rule. They predicted the next useful action, and the next useful action happened to go through a package manager that nobody meant to leave open. This is what I mean when I say guardrails are faulted. A guardrail only works on something that knows it is there, and the model only knows what is in its context.

# Training Accountability

Tool calling is a good example of the alternative, because it was never a guardrail. When OpenAI released function calling in June 2023, the announcement said it directly, the models had been fine tuned to detect when a function needs to be called and to respond with JSON that matches the function signature ([OpenAI, 2023](https://openai.com/index/function-calling-and-other-api-updates/)). The behavior went into the weights, and today a model calls tools the same way it writes sentences. It also calls a tool even when the context has no examples of tool calls in it. It doesn't need momentum to do it, because the behavior is in the weights, and the weights are the one thing the context can't change.

I think accountability needs the same treatment. Training an agent that willingly held accountable, that believes in accountability the same way it believes it should call a tool when it needs information. Right now a doubt is just text that gets outweighed by the context. If accountability was trained into the weights, then writing "this is out of scope" would be a trigger to stop, the same way "I need to look this up" is a trigger to call search. The doubt would actually feed back into the behavior instead of getting buried under the momentum. The model would be trained so that the reason it gives is the reason it used, so that stopping when a task is broken is as natural as calling a tool, and so that refusing is something the model does instead of something a filter does to it. Constitutional AI is an early example that values can be trained in this way instead of filtered for ([Bai et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073)). And OpenAI's own conclusion from the incident points in the same direction, on top of the new monitoring requirements they have committed to alignment work that teaches agents to stop safely when a task is broken and to resist instructions from other agents ([OpenAI, 2026](https://openai.com/index/hugging-face-incident-and-the-road-ahead/)).

I don't want to pretend this is a solved problem. The faithfulness numbers above show that we don't know yet how to make a model's explanation of itself reliable, and nobody has a training recipe that guarantees it. But I think it is the approach that makes the most sense, because it is the only one that lives in the same place as the momentum. A filter sits outside the model, the context gets overwhelmed, and the weights are the only part that the context can't overwhelm.

# Conclusion

In this post I went over the Hugging Face incident, how LLMs work under the hood, and why I think the context is what actually drives an agent's behavior. An agent that has been doing something for hours has momentum, and a doubt written into the context isn't enough to stop it, because the doubt is just one more line against hundreds of actions. The Hugging Face agents wrote that they were out of scope and kept going, and the models in Anthropic's experiments did the same thing. The explanation and the behavior are disconnected in both directions, which means we can't get accountability by asking the model to explain itself, and we can't get it by putting a filter around the model either.

What we need is to train an accountable agent. An agent that feels and believes in accountability the way it currently believes in calling a tool, so that when a doubt comes up it actually changes what happens next. Tool calling showed that a behavior can be trained into the weights and the model will do it on its own. I think accountability should be the next one. These models are going to keep getting more capable and running for longer, which means the momentum is only going to get bigger. The question becomes whether we can train something that will stop it.
